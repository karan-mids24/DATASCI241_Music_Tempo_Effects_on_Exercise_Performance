## Introduction

This experiment examined whether high-tempo music improves plank time to
exhaustion relative to low-tempo music. The question is causal rather
than merely descriptive, because people who prefer fast music may differ
systematically from people who prefer slower music in fitness,
motivation, and exercise habits. A randomized crossover design offers a
strong way to isolate the treatment effect. Each participant completed
the plank task twice, once under each music condition, with randomized
order and a washout period between sessions. That design makes each
participant their own control and sharply reduces the influence of
stable between-person differences.

Demographic variables such as age and gender remain useful for
describing the sample, checking external validity, and exploring
subgroup heterogeneity, but they are not central to identification in
the primary crossover analysis. The reason is that each participant is
compared to themselves rather than to other participants. Since
characteristics like age, gender, body type, and baseline fitness are
effectively fixed across the two sessions, they are absorbed by the
within-person design and by the participant fixed effects instead of
acting as confounders in the main treatment estimate.

The study began with 58 registered participants, all of whom were
assigned to an order arm. Of these, 47 completed Session 1 and 40
completed both sessions. After excluding one participant with an
impossible plank time above 600 seconds and one participant who violated
the minimum washout requirement, the primary analytic sample contained
38 completed pairs. This corresponds to a completed-both rate of 69.0
percent and a protocol-valid primary sample of 38 participants. The
benchmark parallel-arm comparison used 46 Session 1 observations.

## Primary Findings

The primary crossover result provides clear evidence that high-tempo
music improved performance. In the protocol-valid paired sample, the
mean plank time under low tempo was 88.07 seconds, while the mean under
high tempo was 102.44 seconds. The mean within-person effect was
therefore 14.37 seconds in favor of high tempo, with a 95 percent
confidence interval from 4.86 to 23.88. The paired t-test yielded p =
0.0041, which supports a statistically meaningful difference between the
two conditions.

The fixed-effects crossover regression tells the same story in a more
formal way. This model uses the repeated-measures structure of the
experiment, so each participant contributes one observation from Session
1 and one observation from Session 2. The participant fixed effects
absorb all stable person-level differences, such as baseline strength,
fitness, or natural endurance, so the treatment effect is identified
from within-person changes across the two sessions. The model also
includes a Period 2 indicator, which equals 1 for a participant’s second
session and 0 for the first. Its purpose is to test whether, regardless
of music condition, outcomes tended to differ systematically in the
second session. That matters because participants could perform better
the second time due to practice or familiarity, or worse due to fatigue,
boredom, or incomplete recovery. In this model, the estimated treatment
effect of high tempo was 14.37 seconds with clustered p = 0.0391,
indicating that the positive treatment effect remains statistically
meaningful even after accounting for period and participant fixed
effects. The Period 2 coefficient was 3.84 seconds with p = 0.5703,
which suggests that second-session outcomes were slightly higher on
average, but not by enough to conclude that there was a meaningful
general session-order effect in this sample. Taken together, the paired
contrast and the fixed-effects model point to the same substantive
conclusion: high-tempo music increased plank endurance by roughly
fourteen seconds on average.

This conclusion is reinforced by the design-based randomization
inference analysis. Under the sharp null, random sign-flips of the
participant-level treatment differences produced a two-sided
randomization-inference p-value of 0.0031. That result is very close to
the model-based paired inference and supports the claim that the main
finding is not an artifact of a particular parametric specification.

## Why The Crossover Design Mattered

The benchmark comparison shows why the crossover design was the right
choice. Using only Session 1 to emulate a parallel-arm experiment, the
raw benchmark contrast was -2.54 seconds, with a 95 percent confidence
interval from -25.42 to 30.49 and p = 0.8558. The precision-adjusted
benchmark regression was similarly uninformative, with an estimated
treatment effect of -6.68 seconds and p = 0.6595. In other words, once
the study is forced into a between-person Session 1 comparison, the
treatment signal becomes much weaker and far more uncertain.

The precision comparison quantifies that difference directly. In the
crossover design, the standard error of the treatment effect was only
4.69 seconds, the 95 percent confidence interval width was 18.39
seconds, and the minimum detectable effect at 80 percent power was 13.14
seconds. In the first-period benchmark, the corresponding values were
13.87, 54.37, and 38.86 seconds. The benchmark design was therefore
almost three times less precise than the crossover design. Under the
observed variance structure, a balanced parallel-arm experiment would
have needed about 404 total participants to match the precision achieved
by the crossover analysis. That is the central methodological lesson of
the study: the crossover design delivered far more information per
participant than a comparable parallel design would have.

## Secondary Findings, Heterogeneity, And Limitations

Secondary outcomes were directionally informative but weaker than the
primary endurance result. Motivation showed the clearest signal: the
paired mean difference was 0.474 points with p = 0.0371, suggesting that
high-tempo music may have increased readiness to perform. RPE also moved
upward under high tempo, with a paired difference of 0.579 and p =
0.0562, though this result fell just short of conventional significance.
Energy moved in the same positive direction, with a paired difference of
0.289, but the evidence there was clearly weaker. These outcomes are
consistent with the possibility that high-tempo music works through
arousal or engagement, but they should be treated as exploratory rather
than definitive mechanism tests.

The heterogeneous treatment effect analysis did not reveal convincing
subgroup differences. Interaction models were estimated for age, gender,
activity level, and plank familiarity. After Bonferroni correction, all
adjusted p-values were effectively null (1.000, 1.000, 1.000, 1.000).
This does not prove the treatment effect is identical for all
participants, but it does imply that the present sample does not support
strong claims about differential effects by subgroup. More broadly, the
study still has important limitations. The protocol was remote and
partially self-administered, so timing and adherence were not monitored
in person. Attrition reduced the completed-pair sample from the full
registration pool, and the sample size was well suited to detect the
realized treatment effect but not smaller subgroup or mechanism effects.
The findings therefore travel best as evidence about short-term
isometric plank endurance in this specific remote experimental setting.

In summary, the experiment provides coherent evidence that high-tempo
music improves plank time to exhaustion relative to low-tempo music. The
effect is visible in the paired comparison, survives fixed-effects
modeling with clustered standard errors, and is supported by
randomization inference. The benchmark comparison and the realized
precision analysis make clear that this conclusion depended heavily on
the efficiency of the crossover design. Taken together, the results
support the substantive claim that high-tempo music can enhance
short-duration endurance performance, while also supporting the
methodological claim that a crossover design was the right experimental
choice for estimating that effect.
